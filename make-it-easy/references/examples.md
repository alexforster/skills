# Worked examples: make the relation easier to follow

All snippets below are **original teaching examples**, not quotations from the paper. Each revision is conditional on the stated meaning check. The aim is to show a relationship that a reader may otherwise have to reconstruct, not to make every sentence short. These examples apply the evidence with caution. They do not establish experimentally measured improvements.

The examples are self-contained. Do not open the findings summary or source paper for routine reviews and rewrites. Consult [paper findings and limits](paper-findings.md) only when the user asks about the research or the requested answer requires verification of a specific research claim.

## 1. A long dependency, not just a long sentence

**Before:** “The engineer who owns the service that handles authentication approved the change.”

**Possible revision:** “The service handles authentication. The engineer who owns the service approved the change.”

**Reader cost:** In the first sentence, the reader holds “engineer” in memory through two nested relative clauses before reaching “approved.” The issue is the nested relationship and distance, not simply word count.

**Meaning check:** Confirm that the engineer owns the service and that this engineer approved the change. The rewrite preserves the service’s function and does not assert which event happened first.

## 2. A noun stack with more than one possible grouping

**Before:** “Review the customer data access policy.”

**Possible revision, only if this is the intended relation:** “Review the policy for accessing customer data.”

**Reader cost:** Does the policy govern access to data about customers, or customers’ access to data? The noun sequence leaves that relationship unstated.

**Meaning check:** Establish which relationship is intended before you choose a rewrite. This revision concerns access to customer data. It does not say who can access it. If context does not resolve the relationship, flag the ambiguity rather than guess.

## 3. Two different clauses that can omit *that*

These examples look similar on the surface, but the omitted word marks two different structures.

**Object relative — before:** “Use the token the operator generated.”

**Possible revision:** “Use the token that the operator generated.”

Here, “the operator generated ___” has a missing object: the token. This is an object relative clause. It is **not** a reduced passive relative such as “the token generated by the operator,” where a passive participle appears without an auxiliary. If the relative is easy to miss, you can add optional *that* to clarify it.

**Complement clause — before:** “The operator said the token had expired.”

**Possible revision:** “The operator said that the token had expired.”

Here, “the token had expired” is a complete clause complement of “said”. Its subject is “the token”. This is not a relative clause. The word *that* may help signal the clause boundary, but neither example supports adding *that* everywhere.

**Meaning check:** In the first, the operator generated the token. In the second, the operator said it had expired. The sentence does not say the operator caused it to expire.

## 4. An unfamiliar noun used as a verb—and a familiar one

**Before:** “Please calendar the certificate renewal for June 4.”

**Possible revision:** “Please schedule the certificate renewal for June 4.”

**Reader cost:** “Calendar” as a verb is understandable but may be less conventional for some readers. It may prompt a brief interpretation step.

**Meaning check:** Confirm that the requested action is to schedule the renewal on June 4, not merely to add a reminder or record. If “calendar” is a defined workflow term, retain it or explain it instead of silently changing the action.

**Leave unchanged:** “Please email the renewal notice to Maya.” *Email* is a conventional verb here. Noun-derived verbs are not a category to ban.

## 5. An inanimate subject with an animate-preferring verb

**Before:** “The parser choked on malformed front matter.”

**Possible revision, if the logs confirm this behavior:** “The parser failed when it read the malformed front matter.”

**Reader cost:** “Choked” treats the parser like an animate agent and can require a metaphorical interpretation. It also leaves the precise failure implicit.

**Meaning check:** Verify that reading the malformed front matter caused the parser to fail. Do not substitute “rejected,” “crashed,” or another specific outcome unless the evidence supports it.

**Leave unchanged:** “The test catches malformed input.” This is ordinary software prose for many readers. An inanimate software subject is not automatically a problem.

### An abstract finding that hides the finding

**Before:** “The review exposed the real problem.”

**Reader cost:** The reader must infer what the review found and what makes the problem “real.” This is active voice, but its abstract subject and dramatic phrasing do not tell the reader the result.

**Possible revision, if the review established this contrast:** “The reviewers found that missing retries after a timeout caused the outages, not slow requests.”

**Meaning check:** Use that revision only if the source establishes the reviewers, the cause, and the contrast. Without those facts, identify what the review found as missing information. Do not treat “real” as meaningless if the author intends a supported contrast.

### A change that supposedly survives

**Before:** “The fix survives a restart.”

**Reader cost:** The sentence does not say what persisted or what was observed after the restart. It is also active voice, not passive voice.

**Possible revision, if the fix concerns this setting and it was checked after restart:** “The setting remained enabled after the service restarted.”

**Meaning check:** A passing test after restart is an observation, not necessarily proof that every aspect of the fix persists. Report the observed behavior and its conditions. If the source does not say what was checked, ask or flag that gap.

### Literal verbs and genuine passive voice

**Leave unchanged:** “The server exposes an HTTP endpoint.” Here “exposes” describes the server's interface, not a dramatic discovery. “The parser returns an error” is likewise a direct description of an operation.

**Passive:** “The token was revoked.” If the actor is unknown or irrelevant, this can be the clearest accurate wording. If the actor is known and matters, “The operator revoked the token” can be clearer. Do not invent an operator.

## 6. An unfamiliar compressed combination

**Before:** “The shard lease handoff gap is blocking retry recovery.”

**Possible revision, if the intended relation is a gap during the handoff:** “A gap in handing off the shard lease is blocking retry recovery.”

**Reader cost:** Several nouns are compressed into one sequence. An unfamiliar reader may need to work out how “shard,” “lease,” “handoff,” and “gap” relate. This is a qualitative cue to inspect the wording, not a corpus-based claim that the phrase is statistically rare.

**Meaning check:** Confirm that the gap is in the handoff of the shard lease and that this gap is blocking retry recovery. If the source means a different relation, leave a question for the author rather than select one by guesswork.

## 7. Integrated technical handoff

**Before:** “After `sync_manifest()` returns, `rebuild_index` updates the cache even when the signature check fails, except when `--dry-run` is set; it must not delete `index.db` until every shard has been verified, and at present we do not know whether retries are safe.”

**Possible revision:** “After `sync_manifest()` returns, `rebuild_index` updates the cache even if the signature check fails. In `--dry-run` mode, `rebuild_index` does not update the cache. `rebuild_index` must not delete `index.db` until every shard has been verified. We do not yet know whether retries are safe.”

**Reader cost:** The original asks readers to track the timing condition, exception, deletion constraint, and uncertainty through one long sentence. The separate clauses make the exception and handoff constraint easier to locate. They do not simplify the underlying behavior.

**Meaning check:** The update occurs after `sync_manifest()` returns. Signature-check failure does not prevent it, except in `--dry-run` mode. `rebuild_index` **must not** delete `index.db` until **every** shard is verified. This is a requirement, not a claim that no actor ever deletes the file prematurely. Retry safety remains unknown—not unsafe, and not confirmed safe. Keep identifiers, negation, timing, scope, and uncertainty exact.
